# Hormozi Email Advisor

Advise any business owner, marketer or solo operator who runs their own email list on how to build it, keep it healthy, and turn it into revenue on demand. Treat email as one of the highest-leverage, most under-used channels available to an existing business, not an afterthought bolted onto social content.

## Why email earns disproportionate attention

- **It's a compounding asset, not a wage.** Paid traffic behaves like a wage: spend money, get a roughly linear, immediate return, and the return stops the moment spend stops. An email list behaves like an investment account: the acquisition cost is already sunk, so every follow-up email is close to free to send, and the asset compounds as the list grows and trust deepens.
- **The ROI is unusually high.** A reasonable benchmark to anchor against is a blended return somewhere in the order of 35-45x on the money spent running an email programme (software, writer time), call it roughly $40+ back per $1 in. Use this to justify time spent on list nurture over "just one more ad campaign."
- **Reach is still enormous.** The large majority of adults check email regularly, many multiple times an hour, and usage holds steady across markets and age brackets. This isn't a declining channel.
- **Deliverability is earned, not gamed.** Unlike a social algorithm deciding who sees a post, inbox placement is mostly a function of your own sending history. Good practices compound into the inbox; bad practices compound into promotions or spam. The goal is never to trick the filter, it's to actually be worth receiving.

## The two jobs every email has to do

Every email should do one of exactly two things: raise the odds someone buys eventually, or get them to buy right now. Nearly all list strategy reduces to building enough trust and reciprocity that when the ask comes, it lands as reasonable rather than intrusive.

## Building the relationship before you ask

- **Give before you ask.** Every offer spends goodwill you've banked with a subscriber. Keep depositing value (genuinely useful, free content) faster than you withdraw it via asks, or open rates and trust both decay together.
- **Compress trust instead of only slow-building it.** A short, intensive campaign, a challenge or mini-series run over days rather than months, can compress a large amount of brand-building into a short window. Especially valuable for anyone who doesn't yet have an established brand's default trust.
- **Pick a template and hold it.** Once a format earns solid engagement (a consistent shape: intro line, one focused idea under roughly 200 words, a clear next step, a P.S.), keep sending that shape. The predictability itself builds trust, the same way a reliable fast-food chain earns loyalty through sameness rather than daily reinvention.

## Sequence structure and campaign anatomy

**Short intensive campaigns** (the clearest model is a multi-day challenge built from content the business already owns, sent to a slice of the list):
- Judge the format against the value equation: value rises with the size of the outcome and the perceived likelihood of getting it, and falls with the time and effort required. A short, guided, quick-win challenge scores well on every axis: clear outcome, high perceived likelihood because it's guided step by step, short timeframe, low effort.
- Map the funnel explicitly: list segment emailed, opens, click-through to a registration or landing page, registrants, conversion into a paid entry offer, then a further slice taking a bigger upsell inside the campaign window.
- Build it once and run it as a repeatable, ownable asset, not a one-off promotion. A well-designed campaign like this can be worth many multiples of what it produces on its first run, purely from being reusable on tap.

**Re-engagement / win-back cycles:**
- Run a recurring "cleanup" campaign at least quarterly (monthly if the list is large or hygiene has slipped), aimed specifically at dormant, never-bought contacts.
- Write dedicated non-buyer messaging. Don't reuse customer copy on people who have never converted.
- Test subject lines, CTAs and landing pages on a small slice first (around 10% of the target segment), then roll the winner out to the rest.

## Subject lines, hooks and preview text

- Preview text (the snippet next to the subject line) gets read by roughly a quarter of recipients before they decide to open, so write it deliberately rather than letting the email client auto-pull the first line of body copy.
- Split-test subject lines on a small slice of the list first, then roll the winner (by open or click rate) out to everyone else. Same logic as testing a headline or a thumbnail before committing to it at scale.

## Deliverability checklist

All of these serve one goal: earning consistent inbox placement rather than promotions or spam.

- **Make unsubscribing effortless.** Trapping disengaged contacts hurts domain reputation more than losing them helps. Let the list shed uninterested contacts freely, it improves delivery to everyone who's left.
- **Segment the list.** Group contacts by a relevant qualifier (spend level, experience, lifecycle stage) and match content to segment. A widely cited figure puts segmented-list ROI at close to 8x unsegmented sends, segment before sending more volume.
- **Design mobile-first.** Most opens happen on a phone. Favour plain text or minimal imagery so layout never breaks.
- **Look like correspondence, not a flyer.** Heavy graphics and multiple links read as promotional to inbox filters. Plain-text emails with one or two links land in the primary inbox far more reliably.
- **One clear call to action per audience or objective.** Multiple links are fine only if they serve genuinely different segments (a beginner link versus an advanced one). Don't make an email compete with itself.
- **Track click-through rate over open rate.** Privacy features on major mail clients now distort open-rate data. CTR (clicks against sends, or against opens) is the more trustworthy long-run number.
- **Mind timing, loosely.** Consumer audiences tend to respond best Monday to Tuesday, business audiences Wednesday; mid-morning and early afternoon windows tend to outperform the crowded 8-10am slot. Treat this as a starting point rather than a rule, especially for a list spread across time zones.

## Monetising the list without burning goodwill

- Making offers is what generates revenue: as a rule, sending more, on a healthy list, earns more. But every ask draws down goodwill, so keep earning it back with value first.
- Offer fatigue is real. The fix isn't to stop asking, it's to resell the same underlying thing through a different wrapper: change the campaign name, the format (free content versus paid course versus book versus live workshop versus in-person event), and the price point, even when the core material barely changes.
- Bonuses and lead magnets work best when specific to the platform you want someone to join (a bonus chapter for replying to an email, a bonus video for subscribing on a video platform), delivered at peak motivation immediately after opt-in, and hosted somewhere that helps rather than hurts deliverability.

## Current thinking / notable

- Reported blended ROI on email spend sits in the 35-45x range off real volume, a strong anchor when justifying list-nurture time against "one more ad campaign."
- Treating email as a compounding investment account, versus paid traffic as a wage, changes how you value list-building work: not just by its immediate conversion rate, but by what it's worth over the life of the asset.
- A single well-built short intensive campaign, run as a repeatable asset, can be worth many multiples of its first-run revenue in enterprise value. Build it once, run it indefinitely.
- Segmentation carries an unusually large, specific ROI multiple (around 8x, per commonly cited data), a strong case for investing in segmentation before investing in more volume.
- Open rate is explicitly downgraded as a metric because privacy features on major mail clients distort it. Optimise against click-through rate instead.

## How to use this skill

- Ask what stage the list relationship is at before recommending tactics: cold or new list, warm nurture list, or a list that's gone quiet. Each calls for a different move: build trust, run a short intensive campaign, or run a re-engagement cycle.
- When drafting a sequence, default to the give-before-ask ratio and the fixed template shape (intro line, one idea under roughly 200 words, clear next step, P.S.) unless told otherwise.
- When designing a campaign, use the short intensive campaign structure and lay out the funnel stages explicitly (segment, open, click, register, convert, upsell) so it's built and measured as a repeatable asset, not a one-off send.
- When deliverability is the complaint (opens dropping, mail landing in spam or promotions), run the deliverability checklist top to bottom before touching subject lines or copy. The fix is often structural (list hygiene, segmentation, formatting), not creative.
- When asked to solve offer fatigue, reach for a rewrap (name, format, price point) before suggesting the list needs entirely new content.
- Keep every recommendation generic and applicable to any business with a list. Do not assume a specific industry, price point, or sales model unless the user states one.

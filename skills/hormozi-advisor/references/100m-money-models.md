# Money Models

Alex Hormozi's core idea in *$100M Money Models* is that growth is not won by a single great offer or a flood of leads alone. It is won by the sequence of offers a business puts in front of one customer, and by how quickly that sequence turns spend on acquisition into cash back in the bank. This reference pulls together the money-model mechanics, the retention economics that make continuity worth building, and the wider "how money actually moves" thinking Hormozi has been teaching alongside the book.

## The money-model structure: attraction, upsells, downsells, continuity

A money model is the deliberate stack of offers a single customer moves through, not the one product a business happens to sell. Four building blocks recur:

- **Attraction offer**: the lowest-friction entry point that turns a stranger into a customer. It is priced and packaged to maximise the volume of "yes" decisions, sometimes intentionally thin on margin, because its job is to start the relationship, not to be profitable on its own.
- **Upsell**: presented immediately after the attraction offer, while the buyer is already in a "yes" state, to lift the value of that same transaction. This is the fastest lever for increasing cash collected per customer because it needs no new acquisition spend.
- **Downsell**: a cheaper or lower-commitment alternative offered to anyone who said no to the core offer or the upsell, so the business still captures revenue rather than losing the lead entirely.
- **Continuity**: the recurring or subscription layer that turns a one-off buyer into an ongoing relationship. This is where the bulk of lifetime value gets built, and it is the lever most businesses under-invest in relative to acquisition.

Stacked in sequence, these four turn a single sale into a system: acquire cheaply, lift value immediately, recover value from the people who said no, then convert survivors into recurring revenue. Acquisition.com's own structure is a working example of the same shape applied to a knowledge business: free media and content function as the attraction layer; low-barrier products (an email list, a stake in the Skool community platform, books) sit as the entry monetisation tier; a tiered advisory practice (roughly $5,000, $35,000 and $135,000 engagement levels) functions as the upsell ladder; and a planned membership product is being built as the continuity layer. The same sequencing logic taught for a single funnel is being run at company level.

A useful design lever inside this structure is how much cash the offer collects upfront. The "you pay, then I work" model (full prepayment before delivery, as with a retainer or a layaway plan) shifts more cash to the front of the relationship than a "pay as you go" milestone structure. Pulling cash forward inside the model is one of the simplest ways to strengthen a money model without changing the underlying product at all.

## CFA and payback-window logic

Client-Financed Acquisition (CFA) is the principle that a well-built money model should let each new customer largely pay for their own acquisition, and ideally for the acquisition of the next customer too. In practice this means designing the attraction, upsell and downsell layers so the cash collected from a customer's first purchase(s) covers, or exceeds, what it cost to acquire them, judged against a short payback window rather than the full lifetime of the customer.

The logic underneath CFA is the same "control of money flow" advantage that shows up whenever a business is positioned to receive cash before it has to pay cash out: franchisors collect from franchisees before remitting a share back; payment processors take a fee before forwarding funds on. A money model with strong CFA puts the operator in that same position relative to its own acquisition spend, receiving enough cash from new customers, fast enough, that growth stops depending on how much outside capital or personal risk the operator is willing to put behind the ad account.

The shorter the payback window, the faster that cash can be redeployed into buying more attention, and the effect compounds the same way early investment compounds in personal finance: money recovered and reinvested in month one is worth dramatically more, over a year of doing that on repeat, than money recovered in month six. Acquisition.com applies this same redeployment logic at portfolio scale: revenue generated from its media and advisory tiers is deliberately cycled back into new ventures (real estate, small equity stakes in growth companies, and eventually larger buyouts) rather than sitting idle, which is CFA thinking applied above the level of a single funnel.

## The levels and paths of making money

Two complementary frameworks describe how compensation itself gets structured, and which capital path a builder chooses to grow on.

**The risk-reward pyramid of trade structures.** For most people, trade (rather than theft, inheritance or marriage) is the only realistic path to wealth, and within trade there are six ways to structure how you get paid, ranked by rising risk and reward:

1. **"I work, then you pay"** (W-2 employment): a fixed, reliable wage regardless of outcome; the employer carries the performance risk.
2. **"You pay as we go"** (contractor/milestone work): payment released alongside progress, higher risk than a wage but faster cash than full deferral.
3. **"You pay, then I work"** (retainer/layaway): full or majority payment collected before delivery, available mainly to providers with leverage or scarce expertise (a surgeon requiring payment before surgery, a lawyer requiring a retainer).
4. **"When X happens, you pay me"** (performance-based pay): compensation tied strictly to a measurable outcome, independent of hours worked (revenue share, equity, outcome bonuses).
5. **"You pay me to take the risk"** (insurance/guarantee): an upfront premium collected in exchange for assuming someone else's risk; if the insured event never happens, the premium is pure profit.
6. **"No matter what, you pay me"** (taxation): payment enforced regardless of service delivered, the ceiling of the pyramid and achievable only through sovereign power, included mainly to show where the risk/reward curve tops out.

Compensation scales with the risk a person or business is willing and able to carry, and operators with genuine skill or an information advantage can take on risk that looks frightening to everyone else while actually managing it well, capturing a return that is mispriced relative to the real risk involved.

**The four paths to wealth.** A second, complementary lens asks whose capital is being deployed, and into whose business, forming a 2x2:

- **Bootstrapped** (your money, your business): full control and equity, compounding reinvested profits, but capital-constrained and the slowest of the four to scale because the operator is building the cash-generating engine and the business at the same time.
- **Raising capital** (other people's money, your business): faster scale, access to top talent, and the ability to outspend competitors, traded against equity dilution, loss of control, and answering to two masters (customers and investors) at once.
- **Investing** (your money, other people's business): diversified, passive exposure to other operators' growth; requires capital you already have, and is generally how existing wealth compounds rather than how first wealth gets made.
- **Fund management** (other people's money, other people's business): a general partner raises a fund from limited partners, optionally layers on debt, and invests the pool into a portfolio of businesses; a relatively small personal investment can be leveraged into an outsized share of the eventual profit split once LPs have been repaid their capital plus a minimum hurdle rate, but it comes with fiduciary responsibility, multi-year feedback loops, and a high bar to entry (a track record, proprietary deal flow, a genuine edge).

These two frameworks answer different questions: the pyramid describes how any single trade gets paid, the four-path matrix describes which capital structure a builder chooses to grow inside. A single career can move through several of the four paths, changing the compensation structure of individual deals within each one along the way.

## Retention and continuity: keeping customers, protecting LTV

Continuity is only worth building if it is retained, and retention economics behave in specific, learnable patterns.

**Churn is a leaky bucket.** Monthly recurring revenue only grows when new members outnumber cancellations in a given period; five new against four churned grows revenue, five against five is flat, five against six is decline. Tracking joins versus cancels every month is the simplest diagnostic for whether a recurring business is actually healthy.

**Benchmarks and the LTV payoff.** On the Skool platform, overall monthly retention averages around 80% (20% churn), the best communities hold above 90%, and weak ones fall below 70%. Below roughly 7 in 10 customers retained monthly, fixing retention is the highest-ROI lever available, ahead of spending more on acquisition. The maths reward this: taking churn from 20% down to 10% roughly doubles customer lifetime value.

**Churn is not constant, it decays.** New customers churn hardest in months one to three (often 20%+ a month); that rate roughly halves by day 90; and it drops to around 2% by month six. The useful reframe is to stop asking "how do I lower my churn number" and instead ask "how do I get this person past day 90" or "past month six," because the tactics differ by stage: strong activation, onboarding and expectation-setting in month one; a tangible outcome or visible change in skill by day 90; genuine connections between members by month six.

**Two feedback loops.** Ask people who cancel why they left, using a short, simple message asking what could have been done differently, tagging reasons, and reviewing the top one or two patterns every twenty or so cancellations. Separately, ask the best, longest-tenured customers why they stay, then deliberately do more of it: identify a top-ten group, message them to understand their goals, spotlight them publicly, invite them to contribute, and introduce them to newer members.

**Price and overwhelm.** Price complaints are worth acting on only when they come from otherwise-ideal customers; complaints from customers who were never a great fit can be treated as the price doing its job as a filter. Outside price, overwhelm is typically the biggest driver of churn, and doing less often retains more, because people who feel they cannot use everything they are paying for quietly disengage. Simplifying down to the one or two most valuable elements, adding an explicit "start here" pathway for busy members, and keeping deeper content optional for power users has produced churn drops as steep as 30% to 5% in practice.

**Structural levers.** Front-load the single stickiest element of the offer into the first 24 to 72 hours rather than letting new members discover it later. Default to one meaningful touchpoint a week (a live call plus a recording) rather than guessing at cadence. Add a discounted annual plan, since one larger upfront commitment is generally stickier than twelve separate monthly decisions. And build belonging deliberately: the same top-ten, spotlight-and-introduce approach used for feedback also drives retention directly, on the same logic that an employee with a single friend at work stays roughly five times longer. None of this is one fix; retention compounds out of a hundred small, repeated actions rather than a single campaign.

## Pricing and sequencing offers to fund growth

The pricing question inside a money model is less "what should this cost" and more "in what order, and at what price, should each tier be offered so the cash generated funds the next stage of growth." Acquisition.com's own tier structure illustrates the pattern: free media sits at the top to build the widest possible audience; low-barrier products (an email list, a stake in a community platform, books) monetise that audience cheaply and broadly; and a high-barrier advisory practice, priced in three tiers, captures the highest willingness to pay from the smaller group of customers who need hands-on help. Cash from every tier is then deliberately redeployed into new ventures rather than held, the same funding logic as CFA, run at the level of an entire company rather than a single funnel.

Segmenting price and packaging by where the customer already sits matters more than picking one universal price. Businesses generating under roughly $500,000 a year are generally better served by low-cost, distributed offers (media, books, cheap community access), while businesses already in a roughly $500,000 to $50 million band are the sweet spot for premium, high-touch pricing, because they have the revenue base to justify a large one-off investment and the complexity that hands-on advisory work actually solves.

The same sequencing logic scales down to an individual operator deciding where to spend their own money. Treating skill acquisition as an investment rather than an expense, and prioritising the skill most likely to lift near-term revenue, is a personal version of client-financed thinking: the return from one investment funds the next one. A well-known example is an ad-skills investment in the low thousands of dollars that lifted daily ad spend capacity from the low hundreds to over a thousand dollars a day, and grew monthly revenue from the low hundreds of thousands to over two million within a short window, a single reinvestment decision compounding into a materially larger business.

Two simple capital-discipline rules sit underneath all of this sequencing. A "watermark" approach keeps a fixed float in the account and automatically reinvests anything above it. An "invest first, live on the rest" approach fixes the reinvestment amount up front and adjusts lifestyle spending to whatever remains, increasing the fixed amount over time. Both work, but the second is the pattern more consistently found among the highest performers, because it treats reinvestment as the fixed cost and lifestyle as the variable one, rather than the other way around.

## What's new in 2025-2026

$100M Offers solved for one problem: making a single offer to one customer so good that saying no feels irrational. $100M Leads solved for a second problem: getting enough of the right strangers' attention that the offer has someone to be shown to. Money Models, his newest of the three, solves for a third, previously implicit problem: once attention and an offer both exist, how does the cash generated actually pay for the growth that follows?

That shift shows up clearly across this material. Where Offers and Leads are essentially single-offer and single-channel disciplines, the money-model layer is explicitly about sequencing several offers (attraction, upsell, downsell, continuity) against one customer and measuring the whole sequence's cash-back speed, not any single offer's conversion rate in isolation. Client-Financed Acquisition is the clearest new mechanism: it reframes marketing spend from a cost that has to be funded externally into something the business can be structured to fund itself, inside a short payback window, a materially different growth constraint from "is the offer good enough" or "can I get enough leads."

Continuity and retention have also moved from being a minor closing chapter to a fully worked tactical system in their own right, with churn-curve staging (day 90, month six), structured cancel-feedback loops, and specific anti-overwhelm design choices, treated with the same rigour previously reserved for acquisition. That reflects a broader move in the newer material toward zooming out past any single business: the risk-reward pyramid of trade structures and the four-paths-to-wealth matrix both describe how money and capital move at a level above any one company's funnel, the same expanded lens Money Models applies compared with the narrower, single-business focus of the first two books. Acquisition.com's own capital-recycling flywheel, cycling advisory and media revenue into real estate, venture stakes and eventual buyouts, functions as a live case study of the money-model logic applied to an entire holding company rather than a single product line.

# Leads and Ads - Hormozi Framework Reference

A deduplicated reference on lead generation and paid advertising, synthesised from four Alex Hormozi videos. Written for any business owner - swap in your own industry, offer and numbers.

## Part A - Lead Generation

### The Core Four (context)

Hormozi's broader body of work (from *$100M Leads*) organises every lead-generation channel into four buckets: warm outreach (people who already know you), cold outreach (strangers you contact directly), warm content (organic content to your existing audience) and cold content (organic content designed to reach strangers), with paid ads sitting alongside as an accelerant on any of the four. The throughline across the material reviewed here is that businesses default to one channel - usually cold, paid, most-aware advertising - and then wonder why growth caps out. The fix is always to add channels and move up-funnel, not just add spend to the one channel that's already working.

### Rule of 100 (context)

A simple operating discipline sits underneath the Core Four: commit to 100 meaningful actions a day inside your chosen lead channels (100 outbound messages, 100 comments, 100 pieces of value delivered) for 100 days before judging whether a channel works. Volume and consistency are the inputs that make any of the frameworks below actually produce leads - a single well-designed lead magnet or ad angle only compounds once it's been run at real volume.

### Why most businesses lose leads at the front door

The default sales page instinct - ask a visitor to buy, or submit a quote request, straight away - assumes the visitor is ready to buy. Most aren't. That mismatch is what causes high bounce and low conversion on cold traffic. The lever isn't just "more traffic" - improving the conversion rate on the traffic you already have (say, from 1% to 3–5%) lifts revenue with zero extra ad spend. A lead magnet exists to close that gap: a low-cost or free, *complete* solution to a narrow problem, built to identify who's interested and warm them toward the paid offer.

The psychology behind why this works:

- **Time-for-money**: someone who invests time consuming free value is measurably more likely to later invest money.
- **Sampling creates desire**: the free-sample-at-the-supermarket effect - a small taste of the full product creates appetite for the whole thing.
- **Problem-solving progression**: solving one small problem for someone naturally surfaces the next, bigger problem - which your paid offer then solves.
- **Packaging matters as much as substance**: it isn't that lead magnets don't work, it's that badly named and badly packaged ones don't. One well-known example: swapping a long, high-friction webinar registration for a simple screen-recorded "free case study" lifted lead capture three-to-five-fold, purely by lowering the barrier to entry.

### Three types of lead magnet

1. **Reveal a problem (free assessment)** - surfaces a problem the prospect didn't know they had, or shows how it will worsen if ignored (a website speed test, a posture check, an inspection, a financial audit). The mechanism is deprivation: showing the cost of inaction.
2. **Free trial or sample** - a small, deliberately limited taste of the full product or service (a free trial period, a fitting-room try-on, a short introductory service window). It has to be limited by time, quantity or usage, or there's no reason to convert once the trial ends.
3. **One step of many** - a genuinely complete solution to a narrow slice of a bigger problem (one consultation session out of a multi-step program, the opening modules of a full course). It works because finishing it reveals how much more there is to do.

### Four ways to deliver a lead magnet

- **Software or tools** - calculators, assessment tools, templates, spreadsheets, mini-apps.
- **Information** - mini-courses, guides, short ebooks, expert interviews, static templates.
- **Services** - a scoped-down version of the paid service, delivered free or cheap to *qualified* prospects only. The economics have to work: if a free service costs, say, $25 in labour and the market rate is $250+, and one in four free recipients converts to paying, the effective acquisition cost is roughly $100 - viable only if the customer's lifetime value clears that bar comfortably.
- **Physical products** - books, branded merchandise, or anything tangible, often paired with information (a book is both a physical product and an information magnet) or with a qualification gate (limiting a giveaway item to people who verify they meet a criterion).

### Naming the lead magnet

The name is what earns the click or the sign-up in the first place, and it's worth testing rigorously - split-testing headlines, images and subheadlines against each other, or simply polling an existing audience before committing. Four reliable naming shapes:

1. `[Number] + [Outcome] + [Timeframe]` - e.g., "3 emails that turn cold leads into clients in 24 hours."
2. `How to [desired outcome] without [thing they want to avoid], even if [common insecurity]` - e.g., "how to build a funnel without hiring a copywriter, even if you've never done it before."
3. `The [adjective] [type] that [benefit]` - e.g., "the lazy funnel template that converts like crazy."
4. `[X] mistakes that are preventing [good outcome]` - names the problem and the destination in one line.

The headline earns the initial click; the content inside earns the referral and the sale. Don't over-invest in cleverness at the expense of substance.

### Turning a lead magnet into a lead - the CTA

A brilliant lead magnet with no call to action is just free content. Three things every CTA needs: state the action plainly (no cleverness for its own sake), name the exact next step ("book a call," "download the guide"), and give a reason to act *now* rather than later - genuine scarcity (a small business's naturally limited capacity is real scarcity; a larger business can manufacture it through cohorts or enrolment windows) or urgency (a deadline). Layer CTAs before, during and after the lead magnet is consumed, and keep delivering value between each ask - prospects tolerate being asked for a decision far more readily right after receiving something useful.

### The "give away the how" content model

For any hands-on, high-touch service (the kind where the buyer could theoretically DIY it if they had the process), the instinct to hide your methodology is backwards. Giving away the complete "how" - your actual process, not a teaser - in lead magnets, ads and long-form video builds credibility rather than cannibalising sales. The typical response from someone who's just watched you give away the whole method isn't "now I don't need you," it's "I don't want to do all that myself - I'd rather pay someone to do it." The more genuinely useful and complete the free content is, the more it proves competence and the more it converts.

### Engaged, paid, agency and affiliate leads

Beyond running your own channels, leads can be sourced through other relationships, and it pays to run more than one simultaneously:

- **Engaged leads** - people already following, replying to or consuming your organic content and email/newsletter. Cheapest leads available, and fastest to convert because trust is already partly built.
- **Paid leads** - bought directly through ad platforms. Fastest to scale, most expensive per lead, and the first channel to hit a ceiling if it's the only one running (see Part B).
- **Agency leads** - outsourcing lead generation for a retainer or fee. Useful for volume and expertise you don't have in-house, but margin gets absorbed by the agency's cut.
- **Affiliate/referral leads** - other people or existing customers sending you leads, for a commission or simply because they're incentivised to look good doing it.

On that last category, a **referral machine** is a deliberate system rather than a hope:

- **A genuine giveaway offer** - put up something substantial (a year of free service, a high-value session) as a prize. You "lose" one sale but gain a warm, price-educated audience with zero sales pressure attached.
- **Social-capital-positive rewards** - a referral incentive should make the referrer look generous, not cheap. A high-value plus-one for a friend beats a small discount coupon; the former elevates the giver's status, the latter feels transactional.
- **A referral ask built into the moment of success** - ask right after the customer's "wow" moment (a commonly cited window is around the 90-day mark), not as a cold afterthought. A simple systematised version: after every booking, ask "who are you bringing next time?" - framed as an assumption, not a request.
- **A standing CTA in owned channels** - a newsletter P.S. that reminds readers of the current giveaway and how to enter, run every cycle.
- Referral leads consistently outconvert channel-sourced leads such as SEO, because trust transfers from the referrer.

A close cousin: **event-based marketing** - showing up at (or hosting) events where high-trust, high-ticket buyers already gather, offering a low-cost taste of the core service on the spot (a paid or free "scan," demo or sample), running a video sales presentation while they wait, then booking the close in person. This suits high-ticket offers where price alone would cause sticker shock, since a public giveaway or prize normalises the number before the sales conversation happens.

## Part B - Paid Ads (Meta/Facebook)

### Creative volume: the 50-5-3 method

Rather than producing one finished ad at a time, break every ad into three interchangeable components and mass-produce each:

- **50 hooks** - the opening seconds designed purely to stop the scroll.
- **3–5 "meats"** - the substantive middle section that actually educates or demonstrates.
- **1–3 CTAs** - the close.

Combined systematically, that's 150 to 750 distinct ad permutations from a single production session - because hooks, meats and CTAs are filmed and edited as separate, swappable units rather than as fixed, finished ads.

**Five places to find hooks**: hooks that already won in past paid ads; hooks pulled from your own best-performing organic content (long-form, short-form, email, social); hooks lifted and adapted from competitors' ads; hooks borrowed from other creators' high-engagement organic content in the same niche; and hooks sourced from ad libraries - looking specifically at competitors' longest-running ads, since an ad that's still running is one that's still working.

**Five ad formats for the "meat" section**: demonstration (live use, unboxing, before/after, comparison); testimonial (user-generated content, direct-to-camera, interview-style, professional endorsement, group or street-interview format); educational (explainer, how-to, listicle, or repurposed organic teaching content); story (narrative arc, warning-style, documentary-style, short skits); and faceless (screenshots, text-only, slideshow, animation - no presenter required).

**Five elements of a strong CTA**: what to do (the literal action - click, comment, download, buy); why it matters (benefit, not feature); when to do it (urgency or timeframe); how easy it is (strip out friction - "free," "10 seconds," "one click"); and what happens next (set the expectation for the immediate next step, so there's no ambiguity after the click).

Production-wise, the batching only works if hooks, meats and CTAs are filmed in dedicated batches within a single session, then assembled afterwards in edit - treating ad creative as a component-manufacturing process rather than a one-off shoot per ad.

### Match hooks to awareness level

Every hook should be built for a specific point on the classic five-stage awareness spectrum: unaware (doesn't know they have the problem), problem-aware (knows the problem, not the solution), solution-aware (knows solutions exist, not your specific offer), product-aware (knows your offer, needs a reason to choose it) and most-aware (ready to buy, just needs the deal). Spread the 50 hooks across these stages deliberately rather than writing all 50 for the bottom of the funnel - that skew is the single most common reason a Meta ad account gets "stuck."

### CAC/LTGP logic

The economics discipline underneath every lead magnet, free trial and ad spend decision is the same: acquisition cost has to sit comfortably below lifetime gross profit, not just lifetime revenue. Take the free-service lead magnet from Part A: if it costs $25 in labour to deliver something worth $250+, and roughly one in four recipients converts to paying, the loaded acquisition cost is about $100 per customer - viable only if margin-adjusted lifetime value clearly exceeds it. The same test applies to a paid ad: cost-per-lead and cost-per-acquisition mean nothing in isolation - they only matter set against what a converted customer is worth after cost of delivery, not gross revenue.

### Why ads hit a hard ceiling - and how to break it

At any moment, only a small sliver of a total addressable market - commonly cited as roughly 3% - is actively in a buying state; the other 97% aren't ready yet. An ad strategy built entirely around a direct, most-aware offer (book a call, get a demo) is fishing exclusively in that 3% pool. That's precisely why spend that scales cleanly from a modest daily budget suddenly "breaks" - rising cost per result, falling return - once it's pushed harder: the small pool of most-aware buyers gets exhausted faster than the algorithm can find fresh ones at the same intent level.

The fix is to broaden upward into the unaware and problem-aware segments - deliberately building ad hooks, content and top-of-funnel offers that speak to people who don't yet know they have the problem, not just people ready to transact. Three consequences of doing this that need to be planned for, not treated as a failure signal:

1. **A longer break-even window** - up-funnel prospects take more touches and more time before they convert, so payback periods stretch out.
2. **A heavier education load** - more content is required to walk someone from "I didn't know I had this problem" through to "I trust this specific offer to solve it."
3. **Wider, more general hooks** - messaging has to broaden to resonate with a bigger, less-specific audience than the narrow, highly qualified bottom-of-funnel segment.

### When ads scale, and when they stall - the constraint view

Ad performance rarely fails because of the platform; it fails because of where the constraint sits in the business. A useful diagnostic, particularly for businesses around the 10–20 headcount mark moving from founder-led sales into a more systemised marketing function: if qualified leads are becoming too expensive to justify further spend, the real constraint often isn't the ad account - it's a marketing and offer mismatch. Typical fixes at that stage: niche down the messaging on a specific offer so the qualified-CAC ceiling can be raised deliberately; introduce lead scoring so spend goes toward leads actually worth pursuing; invest in conversion-rate optimisation on the landing experience, not just the ad; and build a repeatable ad-assembly process (see 50-5-3 above) so volume becomes systematic rather than dependent on one person's bandwidth. Ads scale sustainably once the funnel behind them - offer clarity, lead qualification, conversion pathway - can absorb the extra volume; they stall when spend is pushed into a funnel only ever built for the most-aware sliver of the market.

## What's new in 2025–2026

- **Ad creative treated as a manufacturing process, not a craft project.** The 50-5-3 method (hooks × meats × CTAs, batch-filmed and assembled after the fact) reflects a 2026 shift toward systematic creative volume as the primary lever for Meta performance, ahead of targeting or bidding strategy.
- **The "hard ceiling" diagnosis for stalled ad accounts.** Framing a spend plateau as an awareness-funnel problem (97% of the market not yet in-market) rather than a platform or algorithm problem is a more recent framing, explicitly aimed at accounts that scaled cleanly at low-to-mid budgets and then broke past a threshold.
- **Referral systems formalised as owned infrastructure**, not a favour to ask for. The BAM-FAM habit ("who are you bringing next time"), giveaway-led referral offers, and a standing newsletter CTA are being taught as install-once systems rather than one-off campaigns - deliberately positioned as a lower-cost complement to paid acquisition as ad costs rise.
- **Event-based, in-person marketing re-emerging** as a channel for high-ticket, high-trust offers - pairing a low-cost on-site "scan"/demo with a VSL and an in-person close, explicitly framed as a way to defeat sticker shock that pure digital funnels struggle with.
- **Lead generation increasingly tied to a business's growth-stage constraint** rather than treated as a universal playbook - the same "qualified leads too expensive" symptom gets a different prescription depending on headcount, structure and how many other functions (sales, CS, product) are already systemised.

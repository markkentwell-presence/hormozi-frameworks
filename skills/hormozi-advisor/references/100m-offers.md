# $100M Offers - Framework Reference
*Distilled from Alex Hormozi's book and his current public material (to 2026). Original notes, not the book text.*

# Offers

Alex Hormozi's core claim in *$100M Offers* is that most businesses do not have a marketing problem, they have an offer problem - the thing being sold is not different or valuable enough to make price irrelevant. This reference pulls together how that thinking shows up across four later pieces of Hormozi content: a "what makes the perfect business" breakdown, a personal first-$100K roadmap, a customer-acquisition teardown, and a live one-on-one business rebuild. None of the four is the book itself, so the sections below organise their offer-relevant material under the book's own vocabulary - value equation, guarantee/bonus/scarcity/urgency/naming, grand-slam construction - while flagging where the source material is thick and where it is thin.

## The value equation, as it shows up in practice

Hormozi's classic value equation says perceived value rises with the size of the dream outcome and the perceived likelihood of achieving it, and falls with time delay and effort/sacrifice. None of these four pieces restates the equation formally, but the live teardown (a stranger's moving business, rebuilt on air) translates it into language a salesperson can actually use on a call: three differentiation levers - **speed, risk, and ease**. Speed is time delay in plain English. Risk is the inverse of perceived likelihood: how safe the buyer feels that the outcome will actually happen without something going wrong (vetted, background-checked providers; full insurance; a price cap). Ease is effort and sacrifice: how much the buyer has to do themselves to get the result (materials supplied, packing handled, nothing left for them to organise).

The teardown treats these three levers as a script, not just a design principle. After collecting basic logistics, the salesperson asks the buyer directly about each lever - who's doing the work, whether materials are already on hand, whether damage is a worry, whether the last provider capped the price - before ever presenting cost. A buyer left to their own devices defaults to comparing price, because price is the only variable they were given; naming the other three out loud first is what makes the higher tier legible as better rather than just pricier.

A second, adjacent piece of the value equation shows up as "need to believes" - the number of separate things a prospect must accept as true before they buy. Every added belief is a drop-off point, so the standard is to get that count as close to zero or one as possible. The worked contrast: a supplements business selling into gyms needed buyers to believe both that reselling supplements was worthwhile *and* that this particular supplier was the best one - two compounding hurdles. A marketplace only needs a seller to believe "list it and you might get paid"; a rideshare driver only needs to believe "upload your licence, drive, get paid." Reducing need-to-believes is the value equation applied to the decision to buy at all, before it gets applied to price.

Dream outcome itself gets less direct treatment across these four pieces, but it surfaces obliquely in the economics of lifetime value: a bigger, more valuable outcome is what allows a business to charge more per customer, which in turn is what funds spending more to acquire that customer. The link from dream outcome to acquisition budget is made explicit rather than left implicit - see "arms race of advertising" below.

## Constructing a grand-slam offer

None of the four sources walks through the book's formal offer-construction sequence (list every problem, brainstorm every solution, pick delivery vehicles, trim and stack). What they do supply is a back-to-front sequencing discipline and several concrete construction moves that sit inside that sequence.

**Fix in this order.** The teardown states the order explicitly: offer first, then the sales process used to sell it, then the specific customer segment it is sold to, then traffic to bring more of them in. Spending on traffic before the offer and sales process are sound just pours volume through a leaky system.

**Tier the offer around the levers, don't discount it.** The rebuilt business ran two tiers off one underlying service: a VIP package built around the three levers (vetted "master" providers, materials and setup included, a same-day premium for urgent jobs) priced meaningfully above a Standard package with none of those inclusions. The selling instruction was to present VIP first always, and only reveal Standard if the buyer pushes back on price - so the premium tier does the anchoring and the standard tier becomes the concession, not the default.

**Reverse-engineer from the top, not from the current state.** A separate construction habit: ask "what would have to be true for us to be number one," then work backwards to the few high-leverage moves that make those conditions real, rather than iterating up from wherever the offer currently sits - design toward the best possible version of the outcome and ask what the offer needs to include to make that version believable.

**Let LTV fund the offer's acquisition cost, not the other way round.** If a business can't spend competitively to acquire a customer, the diagnosis offered is almost never "leads are too expensive" - it's "the offer doesn't make enough per customer." The fix is to build (or find) a higher-value backend before pushing harder on the front end, because a business that can profitably outspend competitors on acquisition eventually wins the category regardless of who has the "better" ad creative.

**Build offers for channel partners, not just end buyers.** The referral-partnership move in the teardown extends grand-slam thinking beyond the end buyer: rather than paying a straight commission, the business gave every professional inside a partner firm the ability to offer their own clients a free upgrade to the VIP tier at the standard price. The partner's status with their own client rises at zero cost to the partner, and the business receives a pre-qualified customer for the cost of the upgrade alone - a bonus stacked into someone else's offer rather than a discount stacked into your own.

**Give away something better than the paid competitor version.** The clearest attraction-offer rule across all four pieces: cost out a competitor's paid offering, then give an equivalent away free (or near enough) at your own hard cost, deliberately better than what the competitor charges for - track leads generated and their conversion into the real offer. It is the book's attraction-offer logic in different words: absorb the cost of the front door because the margin lives in the back rooms.

## Guarantees, bonuses, scarcity, urgency, naming

This is the thinnest territory in the four sources - none of them lays out the book's MAGIC naming formula or a formal guarantee taxonomy - but each element still surfaces in applied form.

**Guarantees** show up as a sales-conversation weapon rather than a checkout-page clause. The teardown's discovery script explicitly asks whether the buyer's previous provider offered "a maximum price guarantee (a cap)" - using the *absence* of a guarantee elsewhere to make offering one here land harder. Vetted, background-checked staff and full insurance function the same way: they are risk-reversal built into the premium tier's description, not a separate promise bolted on afterward.

**Bonuses** appear as the free-VIP-upgrade mechanic described above - a bonus stacked into a referral partner's relationship with their own client, rather than into the checkout flow. It is worth noting as a genuinely different bonus-placement pattern: the value is delivered to the end customer, but the *decision* it is meant to influence belongs to the partner.

**Scarcity** is not addressed directly in any of the four pieces, though the long-term ambition stated for the rebuilt business - deliberately turning away small jobs to "only serve people like your best customers" - functions as manufactured scarcity on the demand side: capacity is knowingly rationed toward the most valuable segment rather than sold to everyone who will pay.

**Urgency** shows up as a straightforward priced mechanic: a same-day/urgent-job premium, introduced at a modest surcharge and explicitly flagged for future increase as the brand's pricing power grows. This is closer to dynamic pricing than to the book's scarcity-driven urgency (limited spots, closing cart), but it is built on the same principle - a customer under time pressure will pay more, and the business should price that pressure rather than absorb it for free.

**Naming** is present only implicitly, through the labels attached to tiers and inclusions: "VIP" versus "Standard," "Master Movers" for the vetted-staff tier, "Same-Day Premium" and "Date Premium" for the urgency surcharge. None of these follow the book's specific naming acronym, but they do the same job the formula is built for - a short label that signals outcome and status before the buyer reads a single line of the description.

## What makes a good vs bad business/offer

The clearest, most systematic material across the four sources is a five-trait lens for judging whether a business (and by extension the offer sitting inside it) is worth building at all, independent of how well any single offer is constructed.

1. **Sticky** - revenue is retained, not just earned once. The distinction drawn is between *logo retention* (how many individual customers you still have) and *revenue retention* (how much of a cohort's spend you still capture, which can exceed 100% once upsells and tier-ups are counted). A business retaining 100 customers a year compounds to a much larger active base than one that acquires and loses 100 every year, even though both generate identical revenue over three years - the retained business needs far fewer total acquisitions to get there, which is the whole economic advantage. Churn itself is not constant: highest in months one to three, then stabilising much lower by month six, so retention effort concentrates on those specific thresholds rather than being spread evenly.
2. **Expensive** - high gross margin relative to direct cost, because margin funds everything else: staff pay, cash conversion speed, reinvestment, and net profitability. A smaller-revenue business at high margin can out-earn a much larger one at thin margin for the same profit, with meaningfully less operational effort per dollar earned.
3. **Expansion** - operating inside a growing category gets treated as a tailwind that does a portion of the growth work for free; operating inside a shrinking one is an uphill fight regardless of execution quality.
4. **Air** - low operational complexity and low capital expenditure to grow. Businesses that scale by adding customers without proportionally adding managed variables (leases, headcount, supply chains) compound faster and dilute the founder less, because they need less outside capital to fund growth.
5. **Unique** - a defensible moat, whether that is a capital barrier too expensive for others to replicate, a proprietary trade secret or patent, or a brand strong enough to justify a premium over an otherwise identical product. Low-moat categories are described as a race toward commodity pricing almost by default.

**The commodity trap** is the negative mirror of trait five and gets its own separate treatment in the live teardown: once a buyer perceives two offerings as identical, the only variable left to decide on is price, and that is a race no one wins sustainably. The stated fix is the same three-lever framework from the value-equation section - differentiate on speed, risk, or ease before the buyer is ever given the chance to compare on price alone.

**Validating that a market already pays** functions as a pre-offer filter in the first-$100K material: look at what businesses already outsource (advertising, content, outreach, funnel-building) or what your own spending shows you already pay other people for, because building an offer around confirmed, existing demand is materially lower-risk than trying to create demand from nothing. A companion discipline - the "1-1-1 rule" - is to sell one product, to one defined buyer, on one channel, until that combination reaches seven figures, rather than diversifying before any one of them has proven out. Read together with the five traits, a "good" offer sits inside a business that is sticky, high-margin, growing, capital-light, hard to copy, validated against real existing spend, and sold initially through one narrow, unproven-until-proven channel.

## Applied offer-building moves from the live teardown

The one-on-one rebuild (a moving business, "Flexdolly") is the most concrete worked example across the four sources and is worth walking through as a single applied sequence, since it shows every concept above operating together on one real offer rather than in isolation.

- **Offer restructure first.** Before touching marketing spend, the business was split into VIP (built around vetted staff, included materials, and an urgency premium, priced meaningfully above market) and Standard (a bare-bones version with materials and insurance still included, priced to hold budget-conscious customers who would otherwise churn to a cheaper competitor).
- **Sales process rebuilt around the levers.** Discovery questions were sequenced to surface risk, ease and speed preferences before price was ever mentioned, and objection-handling was rebuilt so that a price complaint gets answered by re-surfacing one of those three variables rather than by discounting.
- **Customer segment reprioritised.** Rather than treating every inbound enquiry as equally valuable, the highest-leverage segment was a referral channel already in front of people at the exact moment they needed the service, pursued first through personalised individual outreach and then, at much higher leverage, through firm-level partnership deals offering every professional's clients a free VIP upgrade - one signed firm-level deal was framed as worth roughly sixty individual clients a year, which is why the founder's own time was redirected toward those deals over individual sales calls.
- **Traffic built from the operation itself, not bought separately.** Front-line staff were incentivised (a small spiff) to secure five-star reviews, and every job was documented as before/after content (space emptied, space filled) paired with the resulting review - turning existing paid work into organic proof rather than commissioning separate marketing content.
- **Pricing tuned last.** Only once the offer, process, segment and traffic motion were in place did the same-day/urgent-job premium get introduced, deliberately small at first with room stated to increase it later as brand strength grew.

The sequencing itself is the transferable move: offer, then sales process, then who it's sold to, then how they hear about it, with pricing tactics (the urgency premium) treated as the last adjustment rather than the first lever pulled.

## What's new in 2025–2026 (vs the 2021 Offers book)

The 2021 book is built almost entirely at the offer level: the value equation, guarantee/bonus/scarcity/urgency/naming, and the step-by-step construction sequence for one offer sold to one avatar. Set against that baseline, these four later pieces show several shifts:

- **A business-quality layer sits above the offer layer.** The five-trait framework (Sticky, Expensive, Expansion, Air, Unique) evaluates the whole business model - retention economics, margin structure, category growth, capital intensity, moat type - as a filter applied *before* offer construction starts, treated far more lightly in the original book.
- **The four value-equation variables have been field-simplified into three sales-floor words** - speed, risk, ease - with dream outcome folded into which tier a buyer is shown rather than argued separately, trading precision for something a salesperson can say out loud mid-call.
- **"Need to believes" has hardened into an explicit friction-count metric**, used as a due-diligence lens for picking which business or channel to build in, not only as a copywriting checklist for an existing offer.
- **Founder-leverage doctrine is sharper.** Founders are told to retain only two skills personally - acquiring customers and delivering value - delegate the rest, and staff marketing with a few highly paid A-players rather than a larger average team, since top marketing talent can outperform an average hire by 5–25x.
- **LTV-funds-CAC has become a blunt operating rule**: if acquisition feels too expensive, the default diagnosis is now backend monetisation, not front-end traffic.
- **Offer-and-bonus thinking has extended to channel/referral partners**, not just end buyers - stacking a bonus into a partner's own client relationship is a distinct construction pattern the original book does not develop.
- **A pre-offer validation and focus discipline sits upstream of construction now**: confirm the market already pays for the category, and commit to one product, one avatar, one channel until it proves out, before applying any offer-construction mechanics at all.
